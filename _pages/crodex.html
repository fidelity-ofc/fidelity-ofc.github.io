What does it mean to change?

The Codex Cronenberg:

David Cronenberg is a director that keeps popping up on the periphery of movies that I watch. Everyone I know has a favorite Cronenberg, everyone seems to walk away from his movies with completely different ideas. So much of his work was making its way onto my watch list that I decided to actually just pull the trigger and watch every single one of his movies in a row. I have only seen two of his shorts and none of his TV documentaries but I can say now that I have watched all of his feature films. I also brought in other directors to fill out the list and really explore some of the themes in Body Cinema.

This is one of the most valuable cinematic experiences I have ever had, I was surprised at what I found in his works and just how thoroughly he developed some of the same ideas over and over in every subsequent film. I found a new favorite director in Shinya Tsukamoto. I've learned more about analysis of movies as an art form going through this list than I have in all the previous years watching movies combined. I hope that I'm able to convey any of this in the following write-up of the experience.

Stereo (1969) and Tetsuo (1989)

Starting off this journey are two films designed to throw your senses off: one through deprivation and one through overstimulation. both films are also exploring the way that people connect with each other. The scary parts of us and reckoning with those. Cronenberg's film deprives us of any sort of diegetic audio, no score, no dialogue. Just one narrator dryly reciting research notes. I knew right from the start, just from this, that David would be playing with the entire medium. That he would be infusing meaning into the elements of production. Formal. We the viewers are in the position of academic researchers as we review the footage and try to understand the behavior on display in front of us. This film also has a line that I will be revisiting several times in this write-up: 'in order for research to work, the subject must love the researcher.' We also get several shots of people touching by proxy specifically with a medical mannequin and a man stroking the internal organs of his psychic partner through the mannequin. proxy touching and research as worship, omnisexuality and gender, intangible connections between humans and the growth of new abilities in response to the removal of something else (I need to think of a better term for this to make it more clear, in this case test subjects had their vocal cords removed and developed enhanced psychic ability in response. They also were in a setting where all their needs were provided). We get our first exposure to the ideas of Sigmund Freud and Carl Jung in this film and that's going to pop up everywhere through the way he films shadows and reflections. David Cronenberg's very first feature film set the thesis statement for everything that followed. He has been working with these same ideas for 60 years.

Tetsuo: the Iron Man and Shinya Tsukamoto. Why did I pick this movie for this position? Both of these films are an entry into another world. A place where you have to consider new ideas and adapt to new circumstances. We are going to go on a journey with our own body, we need to be thinking about gender, we need to be thinking about romance and sexuality, we need to understand what it means to destroy because we're going to see people give in to their base desires and create beauty from destruction. This movie sets you up for all of that. You have to interpret some imagery about internal spaces and experiences that this movie presents. both of these films actually, they are both right around the 60-minute mark and they carry so much more meaning than what fits in those 60 minutes. Stereo is a great movie for preparing you to psychically connect with the characters in these movies, for you to prepare to project yourself onto them and feel what they are feeling. Tetsuo is a great film for preparing you to physically experience the same sensations that you see on screen within your own body. Don't worry about how clunky that statement is because going to get a much cleaner version from a movie coming up soon (The Brood). I think it is important to have two films that ask you to put work into interpretation at the very start just to set the expectations. It's going to be gross and wet and weird and psychosexual in the movies ahead and almost every film is a love story.

Shivers (1975) and Infinity Pool (2023)

Shivers sends us to another location where all of your needs are taken care of smoothly and easily, just for you. Everyone is on this isolated little resort island and the place is capital R Repressed. Which rears its ugly head as we keep seeing that people end up controlled primarily by their guts instead of their brains and it causes them to give into those repressed basal desires. Just about everything happens in this movie: incest, pedophilia, rape, homosexuality, bisexuality, threesomes, foursomes, food play, it's all there. There's a fascinating bit at the end where bare wooden pallets replace the apartment hallway set and it really feels like you're looking at a bunch of animals locked away in a zoo instead of people in their homes. It is the growth of new organs that spurs all of this on, by the way, still chipping away at that idea. A lot of the characters end up deviating from heterosexuality once their brain is no longer involved with their impulses. David seems to be making a point about 'who people are behind bedroom doors' and also who they are underneath the layers of repression created by the conveniences of society. This movie also gives us another one of the repeated theses: "everything is erotic." That monologue goes crazy, about the dream of an old man explaining eroticism, that even death is erotic (this actually came up in the prior films but now there are words around it and we'll get even more as we get more Freud. I think maybe he's the old man referenced here. You'll see why later). There's a line from the music project Headaches: love is the only thought and pain is the only feeling. It's a good lens to view Cronenberg's stories through because all of these moments of erotic violence in this movie are predicated on feelings of love that are then betrayed. And through that betrayal, new dimensions of their relationship can be explored. This is something that we'll see HEAVILY in future Tsukamoto projects, the usage of shame and betrayal as an avenue for interpersonal growth.

Infinity Pool and Brandon Cronenberg: why did I pick this film for this slot? I was surprised to find out that Brandon Cronenberg was out here directing movies since the early 201X's. I was also surprised to find out that he's making some pretty damn good ones. unfortunately the lowest one on my rating list is the first one on this watchalong list. I like Infinity pool I just don't love it like Possessor and Antiviral. As for why it's here, it shares a lot of points with Shivers in terms of taking suburbanite people and putting them in a position to release some of those repressed feelings. to answer some of those questions that you carry in your gut. How would it feel to kill someone. How would it feel to watch yourself die, to have an analog that looks exactly like you do so you can project yourself onto it as it has horrible things enacted upon it? And it gets punished while you are allowed to indulge yourself in satisfying sensations. And this all happens on an island retreat. We also get a little bit of that exploration of betrayal. I will have more to say about the connections between Brandon and his father in later entries.

Titane and Julia Ducournau. Why did I pick this film for this position? It's a good introduction to my favorite French director Ducournau. This movie starts out basically the same way as Rabid. A woman gets into a car accident which forces her to undergo surgery which causes some unique problems in her life. And the third movie would almost have been an odd man out but Fast Company plays with women and cars and machines as bodies that we see in David's other films. There's a woman known as the fastco girl. She pops up throughout the movie in the form of fastco products. The footage of car guts is pretty damn good which really demonstrates how big an improvement this is Visually on his previous work. The Brood (1979) and Possession (1981)

All right, folks. This is one of the big ones. The first 3 minutes of the brood nearly made me start this entire project over so I could rewatch the previous films and make sure I was paying attention appropriately. A lot of my analysis of those works is after the fact, actually. This movie opens with what seems to be performance artists doing a spoken word poetry dialogue. It turns out to be a public demonstration of a novel type of cognitive therapy where this man takes on the role of a significant figure in your life to let you release pent up rage and process it. for those repressed feelings to physically manifest on the surface of your skin and erupt thereby leaving your body. The repeated line is "I hate you because I love you" And you can insert that after almost every sentence in the movie and see how the character is reflecting it. The psychiatrist guy has a book cover showing a screaming face melding with a fist and it's called the shape of violence or something like that to let you know that people are speaking with their fists what they cannot seem to express with their mouths. This movie is full of unsaid things. There is a repeated symbol with attics or upstairs rooms. They represent the psyche of the people who own them. (for instance the large number of photographs of famous psychiatrists like Sigmund Freud and Carl Jung) It is through this mechanism that we get the number one phrase I will be saying for the rest of forever: "a true work of art serves as a bridge between the worlds of feeling and perception." This is the first line on the back of one of the books in the wife's attic; A Concise History of Modern Sculpture by Herbert Read. This is the truest expression of how these movies make me feel. What the characters feel become things that I can perceive and once I perceive them I feel them in my own body. The emotions of rage manifest as sores which indicates to me that the character is feeling that which causes me to feel that. All of the themes that David Cronenberg plays with run through this filter.

This movie also gives us another major Cronenberg symbol in the form of the deformed children. the trauma that has been enacted upon us becoming visual deformities. deformities that we pass on to our children. these little dudes manifest and are fueled by rage And once they run out, they die. They are constantly popping up around Candice and causing violence that removes positive influences from her life. And we find out that these deformed children are coming from Nola, her mother. This is where some of the beautiful framing of this movie took my breath away. There's a scene where grandpa comes home after his wife has died, he's drunk, he's distraught. He crashes through the front door It stops, drink in hand. There's a snarl on his face We are looking up at him from the eye level of a small child. I felt like a little girl who was afraid her father was about to hit her. Just the composition of the shot carried the information that this is one of the reasons Nola is the way she is. This is where one of her deformities came from which she manifests through Candice, through these kids, the deformed children, separating Candice from her father and her mother and her ex-husband's new girlfriend. Every shot in the movie is like this, when I say a film is bigger than its script, this is what I mean because we never get direct discussion over Nola's childhood in these terms, all of this information is carried through the medium. The medium is the message and all that. I mean this is the first movie where I noticed him using shadows and reflections in the same sense that Carl Jung does. The Shadow of the main character reaching for the door as he does, The way his reflection is behind him like with Freudian talk therapy. When David is playing with Shadow and light, it's likely he's doing esoteric psychology symbolism. By the end, you can synthesize all this information into an interpretation of the finale where we are going into an attic which is representative of the psyche, as the therapist who parallels other characters, with a gun -- with violence in hand -- and we see all of the deformed children in her mind and how the conversation plays out between husband and wife compared to the sequence going on in the attic. The movie is trying really hard to talk to you and you have to reach out and try to understand what it is saying. The Brood was the first movie on this list to make me feel that voice that was reaching out trying to make me understand. Like what you will see in Videodrome from Brian O'blivion.

Scanners (1981) and Videodrome (1983) and The Matrix (1999)

It must have been so crazy to be a fan of Cronenberg in this time period. imagine getting the brood, scanners, and videodrome back-to-back. The man has vision.

Scanners gives us one of the most direct callbacks in this filmography, something we'll see happen a lot by the way, especially as we get to the final arc of films. In Stereo, one of the test subjects drilled a hole in his forehead because he could not handle all of the psychic presence in his mind. The antagonist of scanners did that exact same thing and it was in the confines of a locked down research area like the one in stereo. Like, it could not get more direct. As in The Brood, this movie is very interested in the way art can be used to process trauma and project visions of our self, convert feelings into something perceivable. making that bridge. At one point we literally watch two characters walk into a giant head to have a discussion. It's not subtle. We return to crimes of the Future with a story about a group of subversives. The way those subversives communicate and connect with each other through symbols more than words, through raw projection, with the new potential they have grown. Of course we have psychic awakenings, A little bit of proxy touching, a great scene where they go over the bridge between feelings and perception again as he has to make a man's heart beat faster without touching him. There's a lot of fun work with eyes in this movie as well, something to take note of when examining the artwork in the movie. And what happens to eyes in the final sequence, what they say in the movie about eyes and how you can enter someone through them. It makes you think about how you look at art, how it enters you and you can feel it inside your body. You take on the taboo yourself and you support it (if you are watching the movie correctly I guess I'm sure it's still super icky to some people). The textures are filmed so well that you can almost imagine how all of it feels on your own fingers. I was so in tune with the dead girl in this movie that by the end there is a sensory experience that we are deprived of And the realization of what was going on made me gasp and start sobbing. This movie is so fucking good. I can't talk about it analytically because I just get too worked up about it. I haven't even talked about any of the other ideas besides The Body present in the film. I probably won't. I think if you have really tried to absorb the elements of Cronenberg up to this point, this movie will just deliver itself to you. If you are ready to let a movie dictate the sensations in your body and affect the way you think -- which you should be if you are watching David Cronenberg's filmography -- this movie will bloom in your hands. Just go watch the movie while I talk about The Shrouds

If Crimes of the Future 2 was a cobbling together of previous works, the shrouds just takes that and tacks it on top of itself. They basically look at the camera and say surgery is the new sex. Karsh Still cannot move on after his wife died because he refuses actually look at her dead body physically with his eyes. It is a running theme throughout the entire film that almost nothing is delivered to us directly, breaking news comes second hand, information is filtered through an AI, a solid 5 to 10 minutes of runtime is spent with us seeing a phone being held in a hand while we are supposed to watch what is on the screen. This is digging into that layering discussion we had with Possessor and Videodrome and eXistenZ. You also get hints that he wasn't trying to reckon with his wife's ultimate fate when you learn he didn't kiss her before she died on fear of making her sick. she's going to die, dude. kiss her. There are some truly heartbreaking scenes in this film like the one I just described where he discusses this, one where his wife post-amputations asks if she's too mutilated to be loved. We have that recreation of the sex scene in crash where he is with his wife's sister and she is straight up vocally expressing her position as proxy the whole time. riveting scene, just as it was in crash. This movie talks about the relationship humans have to technology and it's use in replacing our senses. We cannot reconcile with a death because technology is keeping the dead present. We can't seem to connect to each other because we are putting layers between each other. Vital goes the opposite direction where we see how the connection to nature and physical existence saves us.

He's getting pretty old so I don't know if David will produce any more films after the shrouds. I think it is a worthy film to be his final entry and it would be thematically interesting for this to be his outro considering it's about letting go. It also makes for an interesting end point as a movie with a rather sad ending. I suppose a lot of his films don't have happy ones though.
